The trained classifier and the out-of-distribution evidence, in essay form.

What is actually in production.

The production machine learning layer has exactly one bounded job: to distinguish suction restriction from discharge restriction on the SKAB pump, in the specific case where the physics layer cannot separate the two. It is not a universal fault classifier, and nothing in the system treats it as one.

The model is an Extra Trees classifier, versioned as extra trees skab restriction v1. Its saved artifact lives at backend data models skab restriction joblib, and the training report that accompanies it at backend data models skab restriction report JSON. The trainer that reproduces it is backend data train fault classifier py, and the runtime that loads and calls it is backend app ml classifier py. The data comes from SKAB version 0.9, at commit b2c0d46c2971dcbfe71e26087b6d231998bb91c2, which is GPL-3.0 licensed.

The leakage-safe split.

Training uses seventeen independent valve experiments. Fifteen of them are valve1 experiments representing suction restriction, excluding the frozen valve1 2; the other two are valve2 2 and valve2 3, representing discharge restriction.

Together these produce 510 labelled windows, each thirty readings long. Multiple windows drawn from a single run are allowed within training, but every split is made by complete experiment name, so no window from a held-out experiment can appear in its own training fold.

Three experiments are frozen as the final restriction test and are never used for model choice, probability calibration, the confidence threshold, or the out-of-distribution threshold. They are valve1 2 C S V, which is suction restriction, and valve2 0 C S V and valve2 1 C S V, both discharge restriction.

The features available at decision time.

There are six SKAB signals: vibration, current, pressure, motor temperature, fluid temperature, and flow. For each of those six, the detector stores the mean, the median, and the median absolute deviation; the half-window drift and the peak-to-peak range; the first-quarter mean, the last-quarter mean, the delta between them and that delta as a percentage; the linear slope; and finally the volatility percentage and the standard deviation of the derivative.

Every one of those is computed only from the current reading and the thirty readings preceding it. No future row and no evaluation label reaches production inference.

Model selection and probability calibration.

Extra Trees and random forest candidates were compared using leave-one-physical-experiment-out predictions, evaluated at the production detector's actual trigger context rather than at some idealised point. Extra Trees achieved a grouped balanced accuracy of 1.0 in the recorded development run, and was selected on that basis, without anyone looking at the frozen test set.

An isotonic calibrator maps the model's raw discharge probability, and it is fitted using only out-of-fold window predictions. The confidence acceptance threshold is then chosen from grouped out-of-fold trigger predictions, aiming for at least ninety-five percent selective accuracy while maximising episode coverage. The threshold that falls out of that procedure is 0.50.

The calibration set is small and imbalanced, fifteen suction experiments against two discharge experiments, and that is a serious limitation worth stating plainly. A calibrated probability reported by this system is a development estimate, not a field reliability probability.

Genuine out-of-distribution handling.

The saved artifact also contains an IsolationForest trained on in-distribution restriction windows. For every training experiment, an out-of-distribution score is generated by a model that never saw that entire experiment. The acceptance threshold is set at the tenth percentile of those leave-one-experiment-out in-distribution scores, which works out to 0.024273.

At runtime the model abstains in any of three situations: when the exact six-signal feature contract is missing, which is schema out-of-distribution; when the learned novelty score falls below the calibrated in-distribution threshold, which is distribution out-of-distribution; or when the calibrated class confidence falls below its own acceptance threshold.

The same-schema out-of-distribution evaluation uses fourteen different SKAB experiments: fluid leak and fluid addition, rotor-imbalance variants, water-level changes, cavitation, and hot water. None of them trains the restriction classifier or tunes any threshold. The current result is an AUROC of 1.0, with all fourteen rejected. That is a real learned novelty test, though it is still drawn from the same laboratory corpus.

CWRU is a different situation again: it has an entirely different bearing signal roster, so the restriction model marks it as schema out-of-distribution before inference even begins. The general deterministic bearing signature can still handle that supported class.

The frozen result.

Three episodes make up the frozen restriction test, and all three were answered correctly. On valve1 2, where the truth is suction, the accepted prediction was suction, at a calibrated confidence of 1.000 and an out-of-distribution score of 0.0500. On valve2 0, where the truth is discharge, the accepted prediction was discharge, at a calibrated confidence of 0.553 and an out-of-distribution score of 0.0583. On valve2 1, also discharge, the accepted prediction was discharge, at a calibrated confidence of 1.000 and an out-of-distribution score of 0.0632.

That is three out of three overall, with one hundred percent coverage and one hundred percent selective accuracy. Always quote the sample size alongside it: n equals three. This is strong regression evidence, not a general guarantee.

The predecessor that was rejected.

An earlier script, experiment skab classifier py, attempted four-way classification, rotor imbalance, cavitation, suction restriction, and discharge restriction, from a single first-trigger summary. Its best model scored three out of five, and it classified valve2 0 as suction restriction with roughly 0.95 confidence, which is a confidently wrong answer of exactly the kind this architecture is built to avoid. The script remains in the repository as negative evidence, and production never loads it.

The important engineering decision was to not tune that broad model against its five failures. Instead, the production question was narrowed to the only classification pair where machine learning adds value, and out-of-distribution rejection was promoted to a first-class requirement.

What still needs external validation.

Several things remain genuinely open. More independent discharge and cavitation experiments are needed. Natural customer fault-onset sequences are needed rather than laboratory testbeds alone. A locked external calibration and test set from another site would be required to make any general claim. Commercial data-rights clearance is needed for every corpus used in production training. And after any deployment, there would need to be ongoing monitoring of feature drift, acceptance rate, selective accuracy, and the rate at which humans correct the system.
